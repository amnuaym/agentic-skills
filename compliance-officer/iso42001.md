# ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Management System (AIMS)

ISO 42001 is the AI-governance counterpart to ISO 27001: a voluntary, certifiable **management system** standard for organizations that develop, provide, or use AI systems. Like 27001, the unit of compliance is the management system and its controls, assessed by an accredited certification body — not something this skill can grant. It is also **not** the same thing as a binding AI regulation (e.g., the EU AI Act) — say so explicitly whenever it comes up; certification demonstrates governance maturity and can support a regulatory conformity assessment, but does not substitute for one.

## Scope Definition

* AIMS scope statement — name the specific AI system(s), model(s), or product(s) in scope. "Our AI" is not a scope; "the customer-support chatbot built on [model], the fraud-detection model, the internal code-review agent" is.
* Interested parties identified — customers, regulators, affected individuals/groups who interact with or are affected by the AI system's decisions.

## AI Risk Assessment & Impact Assessment

* AI risk assessment methodology — broader than conventional infosec risk: covers bias/fairness, robustness, safety, misuse potential, and over-reliance/automation-bias, not just confidentiality/integrity/availability.
* AI system impact assessment — effect on individuals, groups, and society, analogous in spirit to a DPIA but wider in scope than privacy alone (fairness, autonomy, safety).
* Risk treatment plan with a named owner per risk.
* Statement of Applicability (or equivalent) — map applicable Annex A controls to how they're actually implemented for each in-scope AI system.

## Annex A Control Categories (general structure, ISO/IEC 42001:2023)

| Category | Covers |
|---|---|
| A.2 Policies related to AI | AI policy, alignment with organizational objectives and values |
| A.3 Internal organization | Roles, responsibilities, and reporting lines for AI governance |
| A.4 Resources for AI systems | Data, tooling, compute, human resources, competence and awareness |
| A.5 Assessing impacts of AI systems | Impact assessment processes on individuals, groups, and society |
| A.6 AI system life cycle | Requirements, design, development, verification & validation, deployment, operation/monitoring, decommissioning |
| A.7 Data for AI systems | Data quality, provenance, preparation, and documentation |
| A.8 Information for interested parties | Transparency and documentation of AI system capabilities/limitations for users and regulators |
| A.9 Use of AI systems | Responsible-use policies; monitoring intended use vs. actual use |
| A.10 Third-party and customer relationships | Supplier/vendor AI risk (e.g., foundation model providers), contractual controls |

Map the specific ask to one or more categories — don't run the full framework against every request.

## Evidence Typically Required

* AI policy and governance documentation
* AI system inventory/register (what systems exist, purpose, risk tier)
* AI risk and impact assessments per system
* Model documentation — training/fine-tuning data provenance, known limitations, evaluation results
* Human oversight mechanisms documented — where and how a human can intervene or override
* Monitoring and drift-detection records for deployed models
* Incident log covering AI-specific incidents (bias complaints, safety incidents, harmful hallucination-driven outcomes), not only conventional security incidents

## Certification Path

Same structure as ISO 27001 — Stage 1 (documentation review), Stage 2 (implementation review), annual surveillance audits, recertification every 3 years, run by an accredited certification body. Fewer accredited bodies currently offer 42001 certification than 27001 since the standard is new (published 2023) — confirm accredited-body availability in the relevant region/industry before committing to a timeline.

## Relationship to AI Regulation (e.g., EU AI Act)

* ISO 42001 certification demonstrates AI governance maturity and can support a regulatory conformity assessment — it does not by itself satisfy a specific binding AI law.
* Never state that 42001 certification alone means a system complies with the EU AI Act or any other AI regulation. Name the regulation separately, and flag that legal review specific to that regulation is still required.
* Organizations often pursue 42001 because a customer or regulator asks for AI governance evidence — similar in motivation to SOC 2 or ISO 27001 requests, but the control set (bias, human oversight, model documentation) is different in kind, not just a relabeled infosec checklist.

## Overlap with ISO 27001

* Organizations already certified to ISO 27001 can often extend the existing management-system backbone (shared Clauses 4–10) to cover AIMS rather than building a fully parallel system from scratch.
* Don't treat 42001 as a superset or replacement of 27001 — information-security controls and AI-specific governance controls address different risk domains, and an organization building AI products typically needs both.

## Guidance for This Skill When ISO 42001 Is In Scope

* Ask which specific AI system(s) or use case(s) are in scope before doing anything else — this is the most common gap: treating "AI" as one monolithic thing to certify.
* Distinguish AI governance maturity (42001, voluntary/certifiable) from binding legal compliance (EU AI Act, sector-specific AI rules) — never conflate the two.
* Map the ask to the relevant Annex A categories rather than the whole framework by default.
* Flag human oversight and impact assessment as commonly under-addressed relative to conventional infosec controls — teams often have mature 27001-style access control but no documented human-override mechanism for their AI system.
* If ISO 27001 is also in scope or already certified, note the integration opportunity rather than treating the two as fully separate efforts.
* Recommend qualified AI governance/legal counsel and an accredited certification body before any certification claim is made.
